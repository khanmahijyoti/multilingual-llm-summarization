\begin{abstract}
Abstractive text summarization across diverse languages remains a fundamental challenge in Natural Language Processing (NLP). While recent Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate state-of-the-art text synthesis in English, their cross-lingual transfer, factual fidelity, and semantic consistency in low-resource, morphologically complex languages like Bengali require rigorous empirical evaluation. In this paper, we present a standardized, zero-shot comparative benchmark of eight frontier closed-source and open-weights LLMs---GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-4o Mini, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, Gemini 2.5 Flash, LLaMA 3.3 70B, Qwen 3.6 27B, Qwen 3.5 Flash, and DeepSeek V4 Flash---across 1,012 test items each from the English and Bengali XL-Sum datasets (2,024 total). We evaluate model performance using lexical metrics (ROUGE-1/2/L and BLEU), character alignment (chrF), semantic recall (BERTScore-Recall using \texttt{roberta-base} and \texttt{xlm-roberta-base}), reference-free QG/QA factuality verification (BanglaSummEval), and qualitative LLM-as-a-Judge scoring. Our empirical results reveal a prominent cross-lingual performance gap: GPT-5.6 Luna leads English lexical performance (ROUGE-1 of 24.12\%), whereas open-weights LLaMA~3.3~70B leads Bengali lexical performance (ROUGE-1 of 16.23\%) and factual precision (65.78\%). Proprietary Gemini 2.5 Flash achieves the highest factuality F1 (58.43\%). We analyze the discrepancy between n-gram overlap and factual faithfulness, highlighting key limitations in current automated evaluation frameworks.

\keywords{LLMs \and Abstractive Summarization \and Multilingual Evaluation \and XL-Sum \and BanglaSummEval \and Factual Fidelity.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Abstractive news summarization aims to condense long-form text articles into concise, salient representations while preserving essential factual narrative and semantic integrity~\cite{lin2004}. With the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) based on transformer neural architectures, text summarization has transitioned from extractive heuristic selection to fluid natural language generation~\cite{zhang2019,zheng2023}. Modern closed-source frontier models (e.g., OpenAI GPT-4o, Google Gemini) and open-weights architectures (e.g., Meta LLaMA 3.3, Qwen 3.6, DeepSeek V4) exhibit impressive capabilities in zero-shot task generalization.

However, a significant body of recent literature highlights two major vulnerabilities in LLM-based summarization:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Lingual Disparity:} Benchmark performance remains heavily biased toward high-resource languages (predominantly English). In low-resource and morphologically rich languages like Bengali, LLMs frequently suffer from tokenization inefficiencies, syntactic degradation, and lower semantic coverage~\cite{vanveen2024}.
    \item \textbf{Factual Inconsistency \& Hallucination:} Standard surface-level lexical metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE evaluate n-gram precision but penalize valid abstractive paraphrasing. Crucially, they fail to detect semantic fabrications and factual hallucinations, which pose high risks in real-world deployments~\cite{farquhar2024,tang2023}.
\end{enumerate}

To address these challenges, this paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of eight state-of-the-art LLMs on the \textbf{XL-Sum benchmark} (1,012 test items each for English and Bengali). Beyond standard automatic metrics (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BLEU, chrF, and BERTScore-Recall), we implement the reference-free \textbf{BanglaSummEval} QG/QA factuality pipeline and an \textbf{LLM-as-a-Judge} framework that scores relevance, completeness, faithfulness, coherence, conciseness, and fluency.

The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We establish a standardized cross-lingual zero-shot benchmark for eight frontier and open-weights LLMs across 1,012 test articles each in English and Bengali XL-Sum datasets.
    \item We show that proprietary models dominate English, while open-weights LLaMA~3.3~70B leads Bengali lexical metrics and proprietary Gemini 2.5 Flash leads factuality F1.
    \item We provide a multi-tiered comparison spanning lexical overlap, dense vector embeddings (BERTScore), reference-free QG/QA factual verification (BanglaSummEval), and LLM-as-a-Judge qualitative ratings.
    \item All evaluation code and aggregated results are publicly available to support reproducibility (see Section~\ref{sec:availability}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multilingual and Bengali Abstractive Summarization}

Neural abstractive summarization matured through pre-trained encoder--decoder
architectures such as BART~\cite{lewis2020} and PEGASUS~\cite{zhang2020pegasus},
evaluated predominantly on English corpora like CNN/DailyMail and
XSum~\cite{narayan2018}. Multilingual coverage followed with
mT5~\cite{xue2021}, which extended the text-to-text framework to 101 languages,
and with the release of XL-Sum~\cite{hasan2021}, a professionally annotated
BBC-derived summarization corpus spanning 44 languages including Bengali.
CrossSum~\cite{hasan2023} later generalized this to cross-lingual
article--summary pairs.

For Bengali specifically, BanglaT5 and the accompanying BanglaNLG
benchmark~\cite{bhattacharjee2023} established fine-tuned baselines for
generation tasks, while IndicBART~\cite{dabre2022} targeted Indic languages
through a shared orthographic representation. These efforts rely on
task-specific fine-tuning of comparatively small models. Our work is
complementary: we assess whether general-purpose LLMs, prompted zero-shot and
without any Bengali-specific adaptation, are competitive in this setting.

\subsection{LLMs as Zero-Shot Summarizers}

Instruction-tuned LLMs summarize competently without fine-tuning. Goyal et
al.~\cite{goyal2022} found that human annotators preferred GPT-3 summaries over
those from fine-tuned systems even though the LLM scored lower on ROUGE, and
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024news} reported that instruction-tuned LLM summaries
approach freelance-writer quality. Both studies are English-only.

Evidence for equally strong cross-lingual transfer is thinner. A recognized
contributing factor is tokenization: subword vocabularies trained on
English-dominant corpora fragment non-Latin scripts far more
aggressively~\cite{rust2021}, which inflates sequence length, raises API cost,
and consumes budget that would otherwise carry semantic
content~\cite{ahia2023,petrov2023}. Bengali, with its Brahmic script and rich
inflectional morphology, is affected directly. This motivates our decision to
report per-model completion rates alongside quality metrics rather than
assuming that every prompt yields a usable summary.

\subsection{Automatic Evaluation of Summaries}

ROUGE~\cite{lin2004} and BLEU~\cite{papineni2002} remain standard despite
well-documented weaknesses: they reward surface overlap and penalize valid
paraphrase. chrF~\cite{popovic2015} operates over character n-grams and
correlates better with human judgment for morphologically rich languages, which
is why we report it alongside word-level metrics. Embedding-based measures such
as BERTScore~\cite{zhang2019} capture semantic similarity but compress into a
narrow band and depend on the backbone encoder, so scores computed with
different backbones are not directly comparable.

SummEval~\cite{fabbri2021} demonstrated systematically weak correlation between
these automatic metrics and human ratings of coherence, consistency, fluency,
and relevance in English. Whether that finding transfers to low-resource
languages has received little attention, and it is a question our multi-tiered
design is intended to probe.

\subsection{Reference-Free Factuality Verification}

Maynez et al.~\cite{maynez2020} showed that abstractive systems hallucinate
content unsupported by the source and that n-gram metrics do not detect it.
Two families of detectors emerged: entailment-based classifiers such as
FactCC~\cite{kryscinski2020}, and question generation/answering pipelines that
generate questions from one text and answer them against the other, including
FEQA~\cite{durmus2020}, QAGS~\cite{wang2020}, QuestEval~\cite{scialom2021}, and
QAFactEval~\cite{fabbri2022}. Recent work extends reference-free hallucination
detection to open-ended LLM output~\cite{urlana2025,farquhar2024}.

These pipelines are overwhelmingly English, since they depend on QG and QA
models trained on English resources. BanglaSummEval~\cite{rafid2026} adapts the
QG/QA paradigm to Bengali summarization, and we adopt it here as our
reference-free factuality layer.

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge}

Using a strong LLM to grade generated text scales far better than human
annotation. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2023} showed judge--human agreement
comparable to inter-annotator agreement on English chat, and
G-Eval~\cite{liu2023} applied chain-of-thought judging to summarization
specifically. A growing literature catalogues the failure modes: position and
verbosity bias~\cite{wang2023unfair}, self-preference toward outputs from the
judge's own family, and general calibration difficulties~\cite{gu2024}.
Verga et al.~\cite{verga2024} propose panels of smaller judges as a mitigation.

Critically, nearly all validation of LLM judges has been conducted in English.
Whether a judge's scores remain meaningful when grading a low-resource language
is largely untested, and our results speak to this directly.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Prior work has examined each of these threads in isolation: multilingual
summarization corpora, zero-shot LLM summarization in English, reference-free
factuality metrics, and LLM-as-a-Judge protocols. To our knowledge, no study
applies all three evaluation layers simultaneously to a matched English and
Bengali test set across a common pool of current-generation frontier and
open-weights LLMs. Doing so lets us ask not only which models summarize Bengali
well, but whether the evaluation instruments themselves agree when applied
outside English --- a question our results answer in the negative.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Benchmark Dataset}\label{subsec:dataset}

We utilize the \textbf{XL-Sum dataset}~\cite{hasan2021}, a large-scale multilingual abstractive summarization corpus derived from BBC news articles across 44 languages. The English XL-Sum test partition contains 11,535 articles and the Bengali test partition contains 1,012 articles. To enable tractable, paired evaluation across both languages, we randomly sample 1,012 English test articles using a fixed seed (\texttt{random\_state=42}); the full Bengali test split is used without subsampling. Each data instance contains a document ID, the full article text, and a professionally annotated reference summary (mean reference length: 21 words for both languages).

Generated summary output lengths (measured in whitespace-tokenized words) varied substantially by model. Over all valid completed generations, English outputs averaged 73 words (median 72, range 2--124) and Bengali outputs averaged 58 words (median 57, range 3--273). All models produced outputs substantially longer than the 21-word reference summaries, which systematically depresses n-gram overlap scores. Notably, the Bengali maximum of 273 words was generated by LLaMA~3.3~70B, which occasionally ignored the concise length instruction in favor of extreme verbosity. Relative rankings across these models are therefore more informative than absolute values.

All models were evaluated in a strictly zero-shot setting using standardized instruction prompts. Generation was capped at \texttt{max\_tokens=2048} for all providers; no temperature was set (APIs were called with their default sampling parameters). For Bengali, the prompt instructed the model to write an accurate and concise two- or three-sentence summary without filler. For English, an equivalent English-language prompt was used. For reasoning models, chain-of-thought markup (\texttt{<think>...</think>}) was stripped from outputs prior to evaluation. Post-hoc inspection revealed that 35 Qwen~3.6~27B outputs (8 English, 27 Bengali) and 1 Qwen~3.5~Flash Bengali output failed to close their reasoning traces or left partial tags. For the single Qwen~3.5~Flash output, the stray tag was stripped and the valid summary underneath was retained in the evaluation set. Conversely, the 35 Qwen~3.6~27B outputs exhausted the 2,048-token budget during reasoning and terminated before emitting a summary. Because these produced zero-length outputs after stripping, we exclude them from quality metric aggregation. Additionally, DeepSeek V4 Flash generated three explicit cross-lingual instruction-following failures (1 English, 2 Bengali) where it refused the prompt entirely in Chinese (\emph{``作为一个人工智能语言模型...''}). Because these refusals contained no whitespace, they parsed as a single whitespace-delimited unit. Treating non-summaries as completion failures, we explicitly excluded these three refusals from quality aggregation. Consequently, Qwen~3.6~27B successfully completed 1,004/1,012 English (99.2\%) and 985/1,012 Bengali (97.3\%) generations; DeepSeek V4 Flash completed 1,011/1,012 English (99.9\%) and 1,010/1,012 Bengali (99.8\%). Quality metrics across all tables are reported over valid completed generations only. One Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite English article returned a quota error and was excluded, yielding $n=1011$ for that model.\footnote{The missing article triggered a per-minute rate-limit error; all metric averages for this model are computed over 1,011 items.}

\subsection{Multi-Tiered Evaluation Framework}\label{subsec:evaluation_framework}

Our evaluation pipeline is categorized into three complementary layers:

\paragraph{Lexical and Semantic Metrics.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ROUGE (1, 2, L):} Measures n-gram overlap (unigrams, bigrams, and longest common subsequence). For Bengali text, tokenization is handled via Indic regex pattern matching (\texttt{[\textbackslash u0980-\textbackslash u09FF\textbackslash w]+}); English uses Porter Stemming. Scores are reported as percentages (\%).
    \item \textbf{BLEU \& chrF:} BLEU~\cite{papineni2002} measures n-gram precision with brevity penalty; chrF~\cite{popovic2015} evaluates character n-gram F-score. Both are reported on a 0--100 scale. Literature indicates chrF exhibits higher human correlation ($r = 0.588$) than BLEU ($r = 0.073$) for non-English texts.
    \item \textbf{BERTScore-Recall:} Computes dense token alignment recall scores using pre-trained transformer embeddings, reported as percentage (\%). We deploy \texttt{roberta-base} for English and \texttt{xlm-roberta-base} for Bengali. Because different backbone models produce different score ranges, BERTScore values are \emph{not cross-comparable} between the two language columns; they should be interpreted within each language independently.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reference-Free QG/QA Factuality Verification (BanglaSummEval).}
To assess factual consistency without depending on reference text similarity, we implement \textbf{BanglaSummEval}~\cite{rafid2026} across up to 100 Bengali test samples. Named entity and noun extraction is performed with \texttt{bnlp-toolkit}; question generation uses \texttt{valhalla/t5-base-qg-hl}; question answering uses \texttt{deepset/xlm-roberta-base-squad2}; answer verification uses \texttt{xlm-roberta-base} BERTScore-Recall (matching threshold $\ge 85.0\%$).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Precision (Factual Consistency):} Entities extracted from the generated summary are converted to questions; answers are retrieved from the source article and matched against the extracted entities.
    \item \textbf{Recall (Content Coverage):} Entities extracted from the source document are converted to questions and answered from the generated summary.
    \item \textbf{BanglaSummEval F1:} Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, computed per-sample and averaged across the test set. All scores are reported as percentages (\%).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation.}
Following recent methodologies~\cite{zheng2023}, an independent LLM judge grades summaries for a subset of 50 test samples per model on a 1--5 Likert scale across six dimensions: Relevance, Completeness, Faithfulness, Coherence, Conciseness, and Fluency.

Notably, all eight models hit a strict ceiling effect of 5.00 for English Fluency, which skews the overall judge average upwards. Conversely, Faithfulness scores are uniformly floored (1.10--1.34) by the judge due to strict verification against source articles, uncovering micro-hallucinations and unbacked assertions. The overall judge average thereby conflates a floored dimension (Faithfulness) with a ceilinged one (Fluency).
    \item \textbf{English vs. Bengali Quality Disparity:} Models exhibit higher qualitative synthesis in English (overall averages 3.32--3.51) compared to Bengali (3.02--3.19), mirroring the cross-lingual transfer gap observed in lexical evaluations.
    \item \textbf{Top Performers:} GPT-5.6 Luna leads in English (avg.\ 3.51); Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite leads in Bengali (avg.\ 3.19).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Our empirical findings underscore a critical divergence between model performance in high-resource English versus low-resource Bengali news summarization.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Lingual Transfer Gap:} In English, proprietary frontier models (GPT-5.6 Luna, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite) outperform open-weights counterparts. Conversely, in Bengali, the open-weights LLaMA~3.3~70B and proprietary Gemini 2.5 Flash lead the lexical and factual evaluations, respectively. This suggests frontier models may suffer from sub-optimal multilingual tokenization or reduced Bengali training data representation. The elevated completion failure rate observed in Qwen~3.6~27B (97.3\% Bengali vs.\ 99.2\% English) under a fixed token budget is consistent with this tokenization inefficiency argument.
    \item \textbf{Metric Alignment:} N-gram metrics show weak alignment with factual fidelity. Although GPT-5.6 Luna scores competitively on ROUGE, its BanglaSummEval F1 is below Gemini 2.5 Flash (54.84\% vs.~58.43\%), a gap consistent with entity omission and verbose hallucinations. Additionally, we note a stark inversion for Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, which ranks lowest in factual verification (BanglaSummEval F1 of 49.73\%) but achieves the highest overall qualitative LLM judge average (3.19), highlighting a severe discrepancy between objective factual retention and subjective qualitative grading.
    \item \textbf{Utility of Reference-Free QG/QA Verification:} BanglaSummEval provides an objective mechanism to identify entity fabrication and hallucination without requiring human reference summaries, serving as a vital complementary layer for low-resource NLP evaluation.
    \item \textbf{LLM-as-a-Judge Calibration:} The qualitative \texttt{gpt-5.6-terra} evaluation highlights a significant judge calibration issue. The uniformly floored Faithfulness scores across all models, combined with the ranking inversion noted above, suggest the LLM judge struggles to measure Bengali factuality reliably. This reinforces the necessity of objective, reference-free QG/QA pipelines like BanglaSummEval rather than relying solely on automated LLM-as-a-Judge grading for low-resource languages.
    \item \textbf{Digital Inclusion and Equity:} Bengali has approximately 280 million native speakers and is severely under-served by NLP infrastructure. Our central finding---that frontier proprietary models underperform on Bengali---is a direct argument about whose languages receive quality AI. This aligns with SDG~4 (inclusive quality education) and SDG~10 (reduced inequalities), as equitable access to summarization tools supports information access in linguistically diverse communities.
    \item \textbf{Compute Efficiency:} An open-weights 70B model (LLaMA~3.3~70B) outperforms the largest frontier proprietary systems on Bengali lexical and factual metrics. This is a meaningful sustainability and deployability argument: low-resource-language workloads need not be routed to the largest, most resource-intensive closed-source model. Self-hosted open-weights deployments can avoid vendor lock-in and reduce inference costs for under-resourced institutions.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the scope of our findings. First, each model was evaluated in a single zero-shot run without repetition; no statistical significance testing was conducted across metric differences. Second, the BanglaSummEval QG/QA pipeline was applied to only 100 Bengali samples per model due to inference cost constraints, which may limit the generalizability of factuality estimates. Crucially, the QG pipeline utilized an English-only model (\texttt{valhalla/t5-base-qg-hl}) to generate questions over Bengali text; this remains a significant methodological limitation. Third, the LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation used a single judge model (\texttt{gpt-5.6-terra}), which may exhibit self-preference bias towards GPT-5.6 Luna; a panel of diverse judges would improve robustness~\cite{verga2024}. Furthermore, the judge heavily penalized Faithfulness despite moderate BanglaSummEval Precision, suggesting the judge may be overly strict or misaligned with Bengali factuality. Fourth, the study is confined to two languages (English and Bengali) and one domain (BBC news); results may differ in other languages, domains, or text lengths. Fifth, BLEU and ROUGE are known to correlate weakly with human judgment for abstractive summaries, and BERTScore recall alone does not capture precision-side factual failures. Sixth, as noted, 35 Qwen~3.6~27B outputs (8 English, 27 Bengali) exhausted the token budget and failed to generate a summary; these were excluded from quality metrics, yielding valid counts of 1,004 and 985, respectively. These limitations suggest directions for future work including multi-run significance testing, expanded language coverage, and human evaluation.

\section{Data and Code Availability}\label{sec:availability}

All evaluation scripts, aggregated metric results, and anonymized model outputs are available at: \url{https://github.com/khanmahijyoti/xlsum-llm-eval}. Raw API responses are not redistributed due to provider terms of service.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusions}

In this paper, we presented a comprehensive zero-shot evaluation of eight frontier closed-source and open-weights LLMs on the English and Bengali XL-Sum datasets. Our multi-tiered framework---spanning lexical metrics, BERTScore, reference-free QG/QA factuality verification (BanglaSummEval), and qualitative LLM-as-a-Judge scoring---establishes key performance baselines. Our results highlight that while proprietary models excel in English, the open-weights LLaMA~3.3~70B and proprietary Gemini 2.5 Flash provide superior Bengali lexical quality and factual coverage, respectively, with implications for digital inclusion and cost-efficient deployment in low-resource language settings. Future work will explore parameter-efficient fine-tuning, domain-specific instruction tuning for low-resource languages, cross-lingual transfer methods, and multi-judge evaluation protocols.
